03 Receiver

Noise

The noise that the signal must rise above: the antenna noise over the bandwidth plus the receiver's own noise. With these, the result shows the noise floor and the SNR, so the antenna noise counts towards whether the signal can be received.

Communication Basics

Move the sliders and watch the charts change. Each section explains one idea behind a radio link, from a single sine wave to the bits carried by a digital signal.

1. Frequency and Waveform

A radio wave is a sine wave. Its frequency f is how many cycles it completes each second (Hz). A higher frequency packs more cycles into the same time, so the wave looks narrower; the time of one cycle, the period T, gets shorter. In space the wave travels at the speed of light, so a higher frequency also means a shorter wavelength λ, which sets the size of antennas.

Formulas

s(t)=Asin(2πft+φ),T=1f
λ=cf

c = 299,792,458 m/s. The charts use a few hertz so the cycles are visible; radio waves follow the same shape at millions or billions of cycles per second.

2. Time and Frequency Domains

Any signal can be built from sine waves. The time domain shows how the signal changes over time; the frequency domain (spectrum) shows which sine waves it contains and how strong each is. The Fourier transform converts between the two. A single sine is one line in the spectrum; a sharp-edged square wave needs many odd harmonics, so it occupies a wide band.

Formulas

X(f)=∫−∞∞x(t)e−j2πftdt
x(t)=4π∑n=1,3,5,…1nsin(2πnf0t)

X(f): spectrum of the signal x(t). The square wave of fundamental f0 contains only odd harmonics, each weaker by 1/n; more harmonics give sharper edges but a wider bandwidth. Noise spreads over every frequency.

3. Sampling and Aliasing

Digital radios measure the signal at regular instants, at the sampling rate fs. The samples describe the signal only if fs is more than twice its highest frequency (the Nyquist criterion). Otherwise the samples fit a lower frequency just as well, and the receiver sees that false frequency instead: this is aliasing.

Try a signal of 9 Hz sampled at 10 Hz: the samples trace a 1 Hz wave.

Formulas

fs>2fmax
falias=|f−fs·round(ffs)|

Blue: the real signal; dots: the samples; dashed: the slower wave the samples also fit when aliasing occurs.

4. Analog Modulation: AM and FM

To carry a message, a transmitter varies a high-frequency carrier. AM varies its amplitude, so the outline (envelope) follows the message; the spectrum gets two sidebands next to the carrier. FM varies its frequency, so the cycles bunch up and spread out; the amplitude stays constant, but the spectrum spreads into many sidebands as the modulation index β grows.

Formulas

sAM(t)=[1+mcos(2πfmt)]cos(2πfct),B=2fm
sFM(t)=cos(2πfct+βsin(2πfmt))
B≈2(β+1)fm

Dashed: the AM envelope, or the FM message. β = Δf / fm, the peak frequency deviation over the message frequency. Carson's rule gives the band holding about 98 % of the FM power.

5. Digital Modulation and Constellations

Digital radios send symbols: each is one point in the I/Q plane (the amplitude and phase of the carrier) and carries k = log₂ M bits. The constellation diagram shows all M points with the bits each represents; Gray coding gives neighbouring points bit patterns that differ in one bit, so the most likely mistake costs only one bit. More points carry more bits per symbol, but they sit closer together, so noise pushes the received point across a boundary more easily.

Green points are the symbols your bits use. Blue dots are received symbols after noise; orange ones crossed into a neighbour's area and were decoded wrongly. For 64QAM, point at a symbol to see its bits.

6. The Noise Floor

Even with no signal, a receiver sees noise. The antenna picks up thermal noise from whatever it looks at, measured as its antenna noise temperature Ta: about 290 K facing the ground or buildings, as little as 10–50 K for a dish facing a clear sky. The receiver adds its own noise, set by its noise figure. Their sum, over the receiver bandwidth, is the noise floor: a signal must rise above it by the required SNR to be decoded. A wider bandwidth collects more noise.

From a spectrum analyzer reading

Connect the antenna to a spectrum analyzer with no signal present. The analyzer shows the noise in its resolution bandwidth (RBW), so convert the reading to a density first. Terminate the input with 50 Ω and read again: if the level barely changes with the antenna connected, the analyzer's own noise hides the antenna noise, so turn on its preamplifier or add an LNA at the antenna.

Formulas

Log averaging reads noise about 2.5 dB low; a noise marker corrects it. In the Link Budget, the same calculation is under Noise and SNR, with an estimator of the antenna temperature from the sky.

7. Antenna Gain: Signal versus Noise

A high-gain antenna makes a weak satellite signal stand out, yet it does not raise the noise by the same amount. The signal comes from one direction and arrives like evenly falling rain: a higher-gain antenna is a bigger bucket, with an effective area Ae proportional to its gain, so it collects more of it. The noise is the heat radiation of everything the antenna sees, and depends on how warm that is, not on how much of it: a narrow beam sees a smaller patch but is more sensitive towards it, and the two cancel. The antenna noise is set by the average temperature inside the beam, Ta.

Try the box first: the signal keeps rising with the gain while the noise does not move. Untick it: an antenna facing the sky sees less warm ground as its beam narrows, so the noise even falls. This is why satellite ground stations use large dishes, and why they are rated by G/T.

Formulas

Ae=Gλ24π,P=S·Ae
Ta=14π∮G(θ,φ)T(θ,φ)dΩ,14π∮GdΩ=1,N=kTaB

S: power flux density of the signal (W/m²); G(θ, φ): antenna gain towards each direction; T(θ, φ): temperature of what lies in that direction. The gain averages to 1 over all directions, so a uniform 290 K scene gives Ta = 290 K whatever the gain. DVB-S2 Transmitter and Receiver

DVB-S2 (ETSI EN 302 307-1) is the satellite broadcasting and broadband standard behind most digital satellite TV and many data links. Follow a stream through every stage of the transmitter and back out of the receiver. The settings below apply to every section, and each figure follows them.

Transmission settings

Select a block to jump to its section. The transmitter is fully specified by the standard; the receiver is up to each manufacturer, so its stages show the usual techniques.

Transmitter

1. Mode adaptation: input stream to BBHEADER and DATA FIELD

The input is usually an MPEG transport stream of 188-byte packets. For adaptive coding (ACM), null packets (PID 8191) can be removed and re-inserted at the receiver. Each packet's sync byte is replaced by a CRC-8 of the previous packet, so the receiver can check every packet. The stream is then sliced into DATA FIELDs, and an 80-bit BBHEADER in front of each tells the receiver how to rebuild the stream.

2. Stream adaptation: padding and BB scrambling

Zero bits pad the BBFRAME up to Kbch bits when the DATA FIELD is shorter. The whole BBFRAME is then XORed with a pseudo-random sequence, restarted at every frame, so long runs of equal bits do not concentrate the spectrum (energy dispersal). Enter some bits to see them scrambled; the receiver applies the same sequence again to restore them.

Formulas

1+X14+X15

A 15-stage shift register loaded with 100101010000000 at the start of every BBFRAME; its output is XORed with the data from the first bit to bit Kbch.

3. FEC encoding: BCH outer code and LDPC inner code

Two error-correcting codes protect each BBFRAME. The LDPC code does most of the work: its long 64 800-bit block (or 16 200 bits) comes within about 0.7 to 1 dB of the Shannon limit. The small BCH code outside it corrects the few errors the LDPC decoder may leave, so the output is quasi error free (PER 10−7). Both are systematic: the data bits pass unchanged and parity bits are appended.

4. Bit interleaving (8PSK, 16APSK, 32APSK)

The FECFRAME is written into a block interleaver column by column and read out row by row. Each modulation symbol therefore takes its bits from different parts of the codeword, so a symbol hit by noise damages bits the LDPC decoder can correct separately. QPSK, whose two bits are equally reliable, is not interleaved.

Formulas

column=⌊k/R⌋,row=kmodR,R=nldpc/C

Bit k of the FECFRAME is written to row k mod R of column ⌊k / R⌋; the rows are read out in order, one symbol's bits per row. R = nldpc / C rows and C columns (table 8).

5. Mapping into the constellation

Groups of 2 to 5 bits select one point: Gray-coded QPSK and 8PSK, or amplitude and phase shift keying (APSK) with points on 2 or 3 rings. APSK suits satellite amplifiers, which compress high amplitudes: fewer rings than square QAM keep the signal closer to constant envelope. The ring ratio γ depends on the code rate (tables 9 and 10).

6. Physical layer framing: PLHEADER, pilots and PL scrambling

The symbols are cut into SLOTs of 90 symbols. A one-slot PLHEADER goes first: a 26-symbol start-of-frame (SOF) pattern the receiver searches for, and a 64-symbol PLS code carrying the MODCOD and frame type, strongly protected and sent in robust π/2-BPSK. Optional pilot blocks of 36 known symbols every 16 slots help the receiver track the carrier phase. Finally every symbol after the header is rotated by a known pseudo-random multiple of 90° (PL scrambling).

7. Baseband shaping and quadrature modulation

Each symbol is shaped by a square-root raised cosine (SRRC) filter, which keeps the spectrum within Rs(1 + α). The receiver applies the same filter, so the pair forms a raised cosine with no intersymbol interference. A smaller roll-off α packs carriers closer together. I and Q then modulate the carrier.

Receiver

1. Front end and matched filter

The LNB on the dish amplifies the signal and converts it to L-band (950 to 2150 MHz). The tuner brings it to baseband I and Q, automatic gain control sets the level, and an ADC samples it, usually at two samples per symbol or more. A matched filter, the same SRRC as the transmitter's, maximizes the SNR at the sampling instants.

The SRRC pulse alone (dashed) is not zero at the other symbol instants, so it would leave intersymbol interference; after the matched filter the raised cosine (thick) crosses zero at every other instant (dots), and each sample holds one symbol only. The pulses follow the roll-off chosen at the top.

Formulas

y(t)=r(t)*h(−t)

r(t): received baseband signal; h(t): SRRC impulse response. The cascade of the two SRRC filters is a raised cosine, which is zero at every other symbol instant.

2. Timing recovery and frame synchronization

A timing error detector, such as Gardner's, compares samples halfway between symbols to adjust the sampling instant. The receiver then correlates the incoming symbols with the known 26-symbol SOF to find each frame. Correlating with differences of consecutive symbols keeps this working before the carrier frequency is known. It then decodes the PLS code, choosing the most likely of its 128 codewords, to learn the MODCOD, the frame length and whether pilots follow.

3. Carrier recovery and PL descrambling

Doppler and LNB drift leave a frequency offset, and oscillator phase noise rotates the constellation. The known PLHEADER and pilot symbols reveal the offset and phase, which are corrected before decoding. PL descrambling multiplies by the conjugate of the scrambling sequence. Move the phase error and see the constellation rotate until the pilots correct it.

4. Soft demapping and bit deinterleaving

Instead of deciding each bit as 0 or 1, the demapper computes how likely each value is: a log-likelihood ratio (LLR), positive when 0 is more likely, with a magnitude showing the confidence. Points close to a decision boundary give small LLRs. The LLRs are deinterleaved back into codeword order for the LDPC decoder.

Each square is a possible received point, coloured by the LLR of the chosen bit for the selected MODCOD: blue where 0 is more likely, orange where 1 is, paler near the boundary, where the LLR is small and the decoder trusts the bit less. A higher Es/N₀ makes the same distance more convincing, so the colours sharpen.

5. LDPC and BCH decoding

The LDPC decoder passes messages between the bit nodes and the check nodes of its sparse parity check matrix, refining the LLRs each iteration, typically up to 50 iterations, until every parity check is satisfied. The BCH decoder then corrects the up to t residual bit errors. Table 13's Es/N₀ values are the levels at which this chain reaches quasi error free operation.

A Tanner graph: each check (square) joins the bits (circles) its row of H adds up, and messages travel along the edges. This toy code has 12 bits; DVB-S2 has 64 800 or 16 200, each in 2 to 13 checks.

6. Stream recovery

The BB descrambler removes the PRBS, and the BBHEADER is checked with its CRC-8 and read: DFL gives the DATA FIELD length, SYNCD where the first packet starts, UPL the packet length. Each packet's CRC-8 is checked and the sync byte restored, deleted null packets are re-inserted at their original places, and the transport stream leaves the receiver for the decoder.
